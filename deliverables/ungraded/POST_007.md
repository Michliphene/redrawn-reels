## Description (YouTube)
```
Rereading feels like learning. In this study, recalling is what lasted.

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (Washington University in St. Louis) gave 180 students a short science text. One group studied it in four 5-minute sessions (about 14 reads). Another read it once, then wrote down everything they could remember, three times, with no corrections. Tested five minutes later, the rereaders recalled more (83% vs. 71%), and they felt surer they would remember it next week. Tested a week later (other students, same methods), it flipped: 40% for rereading, 61% for repeated recall.

Careful: one lab, college students, short texts, and the final test was recall of the same passage. But a 2013 review of ten study techniques (Dunlosky et al.) rated practice testing "high utility" and rereading "low".

Try it: close the book, write what you remember, then check what you missed.

Sources
- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255.
- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58.
```
