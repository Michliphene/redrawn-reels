## Why this case (selection, 18 Nov 2026) — "a theme that speaks to even more people"
Compared for reach: Alcatraz 1962, the Louvre crown jewels 2025, the Great Train Robbery 1963, the Gardner Museum 1990,
the Mona Lisa 1911.
- **Recognition: maximal.** Everybody on Earth knows the painting, at any age and in any country. No other case starts
  with a face the viewer already knows. Frame 0 is the Mona Lisa itself (public-domain photo, LoC).
- **Myth-bust twist (a sendable fact):** "she wasn't that famous before she was stolen". Many historians credit the theft
  with part of her fame. The reel says it this way ("isn't that famous yet") and never claims a single cause.
- **Topical:** the Louvre robbery of Oct 2025 put the museum's security in the news worldwide. It is not used on screen
  (that case isn't tried), but it keeps the search demand high.
- **Kid-safe, no violence, a famous cameo (Picasso questioned)**: the widest possible audience for the channel.
- Rejected: Alcatraz (US-centric, the same "never found" ending as CASE 001), Louvre 2025 (not tried, facts still moving),
  Great Train Robbery (a driver badly hurt; UK-centric), Gardner (the painting nobody knows).

## Hooks considered
1. "20,000 a day come to see her. One man took her." ✔ (number + contradiction; the face does the rest)
2. "The most famous painting in the world wasn't famous… until it was stolen." (gives the twist away in second 1)
3. "In 1911, a handyman walks out of the Louvre with the Mona Lisa under his clothes." (no number)

## Script (voice, 13 beats)
1. 20,000 a day come to see her. One man took her.
2. Paris, 1911. Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian handyman, once worked at the Louvre. He even helped fit her protective glass.
3. On August 21, he lifts her off the wall, hides her under his clothes, and walks out.
4. Nobody notices for over a day.
5. The Louvre closes for a week. When it reopens, crowds line up… to stare at an empty wall.
6. And here's the twist. In 1911, she isn't that famous yet. Admired, yes. A superstar? Not yet.
7. Then the theft hits front pages around the world.
8. Police arrest the poet Apollinaire. They question Pablo Picasso. Both are cleared.
9. All along, she's in Peruggia's room in Paris. In a trunk. For over two years.
10. December 1913. He takes her to Florence and offers her to an art dealer.
11. The dealer brings the director of the Uffizi gallery. Then the police. Caught.
12. In Italy, some cheer him as a patriot. He's out within months.
13. She goes back to the Louvre. And the whole world comes to see her.
(Loop: "…the whole world comes to see her." → "20,000 a day come to see her.")

## Debated points (what the reel must NOT state as fact)
1. **"The theft made her famous."** Historians agree it contributed hugely; how much is debated (she was admired before).
   The voice says "she isn't that famous yet" / "now the whole world comes to see her" — no "because of him" claim.
2. **His motive** (patriotism vs money): "some cheer him as a patriot" is a fact about the reception, not about his motive.
3. **Exact hours / entry the night before / the smock:** not used. One line on how he left (under his clothes).
4. **Sentence length:** 1 year 15 days reduced to about 7 months (some say 8): "out within months" + ACCOUNTS VARY.
5. **The arrest day** (11 or 12 Dec 1913): screen shows DEC 1913 only.
6. **Map:** modern borders as a base map, no border labelled; the train route is drawn APPROX. (Paris–Lyon–Turin–Florence corridor),
   not a documented itinerary.
